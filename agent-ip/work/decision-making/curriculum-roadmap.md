# Curriculum roadmap: Decision-Making & Professional Judgment

## In one paragraph
Fellows practise making clear, defensible decisions at work: judging a decision by its reasoning rather than its luck, naming what a choice costs, sizing risk, deciding when information is missing, choosing what to do first, catching four common thinking traps, and handling ethical grey areas. Every module uses scenarios from remote teams, freelance clients, startups and NGOs, and ends with a short piece of written evidence about the fellow's own work or study. At the end, each fellow writes a one-page decision memo about a real decision of their own and answers a mentor's questions about it. This roadmap describes the course as already drafted (7 modules, 36 chapters, about 10 hours); it was written after the chapters, so instructors are confirming an existing shape, not a plan.

## Learner and entry prerequisites
- Learner: fellows in Gaza building tech careers (students, junior developers, designers, freelancers). English is a second language. Most learn on a phone, with unreliable power and internet.
- Prior knowledge: none in decision-making. Fellows need to read short, plain English and have some work, study or project experience to draw real decisions from.
- Time available: about 10 hours of course time, done in sessions of 1.5 to 3 hours, two sessions a week, over two to three weeks including mentor feedback.
- Entry diagnostic: none in the draft. The open question in chapter 1.1 (describe a recent decision of your own) acts as an informal baseline the capstone can be compared against.

## Capstone
- What the learner submits: a one-page decision memo (250 to 600 words, minimum 250) about a real decision of their own, in eight parts: the decision with a date, two to four options, three to five weighted criteria, the trade-off, the top two or three risks with likelihood, impact and plan, the choice and why, what would change their mind, and a review date with what they will check. Optional: a 3 to 5 minute audio or video link explaining it as if to a team lead.
- How a mentor reviews it: the mentor judges reasoning, not whether the choice is "right". They check the 7.3 self-check table: one clear question with a date, at least two real options, criteria that the reasons actually use, a named cost, risks with plans, and a checkable "change my mind" line. The mentor replies with feedback and questions, not a score.
- Defense or follow-up questions (suggested; the draft does not script them): Which option did you almost choose, and what would have made it win? What does your choice cost you? Which risk worries you most, and what is your plan? Did you look for evidence against your choice? What fact, number or date would change your mind? On your review date, how will you separate your reasoning from luck?
- Submission requirements: written memo required; recording optional and never a replacement for the memo; a real, current decision of the learner's own; no passwords, private client data or secrets, and names changed where needed; any recording link must open without signing in.

## Variants
None planned. Soft-skills track; see open decisions.

## Out of scope
- Quantitative decision analysis (expected value, probability, decision trees). Too heavy for a 10-hour beginner course in a second language; the weighted matrix and low/medium/high ratings are enough to reason clearly.
- Group decision methods and facilitation beyond the pre-mortem. Team process belongs to the Collaboration course.
- Negotiation, managing expectations and delivering bad news in depth. Covered by the Collaboration and Business Communication courses; this course only teaches the decision of what to drop and a short message shape.
- A full catalogue of cognitive biases. Four common traps taught well beat a long list.
- Legal advice on contracts, data protection or whistleblowing. The ethics module teaches judgment and escalation, not law.
- Personal life, financial or career advice. Learners apply tools to their own decisions; mentors review reasoning, not the choice.
- Formal technical decision records (ADRs, architecture reviews). Technical choices appear only as scenarios.
