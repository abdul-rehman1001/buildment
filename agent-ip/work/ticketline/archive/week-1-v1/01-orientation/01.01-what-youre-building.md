## Two buyers, one ticket

At 10:00:00.000, a 900-seat venue has one ticket left. Two buyers press **Pay** within 180 milliseconds of each other. Both requests reach your API. Both see one available ticket. Both write an order.

One of those buyers will arrive at the venue with a valid-looking ticket and no seat. The other will discover the problem at the gate. The refund is annoying; the trust damage is worse.

This is the moment Ticketline exists to survive. Everything else — the event page, the organizer dashboard, the QR code, the receipt — is scaffolding around that moment. If your system cannot answer "who gets the last ticket?" correctly, it is a demo, not a ticketing platform.

## What you are building

Ticketline is a ticketing platform for small and mid-sized venues. An organizer publishes an event with ticket types and a capacity. A buyer holds inventory, pays with Stripe test mode, and receives a QR ticket. Gate staff scan the ticket at the door. The organizer sees sales and check-ins.

You are not building a Ticketmaster clone. You are building the smallest system that can honestly demonstrate the hard parts: concurrency, payments, idempotency, access control, and offline reconciliation. The course cuts features aggressively so those parts get the attention they deserve.

## The four invariants

Write these down. You will test them, break them, and defend them.

1. **No overselling.** `sold_quantity + held_quantity <= capacity` for every ticket type, at every instant.
2. **One paid order, one set of tickets.** A duplicate payment event must not create a second order or a second ticket.
3. **One ticket, one entry.** A ticket admits one attendee once. A duplicate scan is recorded and rejected.
4. **One organizer, one event.** An organizer can read and mutate only their own events. A gate staff member can scan only assigned events.

Each invariant has a failure story. A sold-out seat with two buyers. A webhook that arrives twice. A ticket screenshot shared with a friend. An event ID changed in a URL. The course is a sequence of decisions that make those stories impossible.

## The five-minute demo

By the end of the course, you will run this demo without notes:

1. An organizer creates and publishes an event with two ticket types and a capacity.
2. Two buyers race for the last ticket. One gets a hold; the other gets a clear conflict.
3. The winner pays with a Stripe test card. A webhook confirms the order and issues a QR ticket.
4. A gate staff member scans the ticket. A second scan is rejected as a duplicate.
5. The scanner goes offline, checks in another ticket, reconnects, and syncs without duplication.
6. The organizer dashboard shows sales, capacity, and check-in counts.

That demo is the course's acceptance test. If any step is fake — a hard-coded order, a disabled button instead of a server check, a scan that does not record a result — you have not finished.

## What this course is not

- **Not a real payment product.** You will use Stripe test mode. No real money moves.
- **Not a feature checklist.** You will not build dynamic pricing, resale, transfers, or a native app.
- **Not a copy-paste tutorial.** You get contracts, schemas, acceptance criteria, and failure tests. You write the implementation.
- **Not a theory course.** Progress is tied to working software and evidence, not pages read.

## Key ideas

- Ticketline is organized around one hard promise: a trustworthy last-ticket purchase.
- The four invariants are no overselling, one paid order, one ticket one entry, and one organizer one event.
- The demo is the acceptance test, not a marketing script.
- The course is a guided build: you write the implementation and prove it with tests.
